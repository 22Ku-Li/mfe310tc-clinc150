Abstract—We compare TF-IDF logistic regression (M0), a
compact TextCNN (M1), and a controlled R-Drop variant
(M2) for 150-intent classification and out-of-scope (OOS)
detection on CLINC150. M0 gives the highest test Macro-F1
(0.8508) and in-scope Macro-F1 (0.9148), but catches only
27.1% of unsupported requests. M1 and M2 raise mean OOS
recall to 43.7% and 42.3%, with OOS F1 of 0.5862 and
0.5746. R-Drop raises mean test Macro-F1 from 0.8293 to
0.8343; the paired difference is positive for all three seeds. It
also improves Macro-F1 under 5% and 10% spelling noise,
although M0 remains more robust overall. All experiments
were reproduced on CPU. The approved remote Linux GPU
requirement is not met.
Keywords—intent classification, out-of-scope detection,
CLINC150, TextCNN, R-Drop, robustness
I. INTRODUCTION
A reliable assistant should do more than route supported
commands to the right function. It should also recognize
when a request falls outside its scope. For a factory worker
asking about maintenance records, machine status, or an
operating instruction, either kind of failure matters. A wrong
label can lead to an irrelevant function, while accepting an
unsupported command can suggest that the assistant can do
something it cannot. We therefore evaluate intent
classification and OOS behaviour together.
We use CLINC150 as a controlled benchmark for this
study[1]. It is not a factory corpus, and we do not treat
benchmark performance as evidence of industrial readiness.
The manufacturing setting simply gives us a concrete way to
examine reliability. Our experiments ask three questions:
how well a transparent baseline works, whether a small
convolutional network offers a different accuracy–OOS
trade-off, and whether one controlled R-Drop change
improves clean and noisy performance.
We initially planned to use DistilBERT, but could not
confirm access to an approved GPU terminal. We therefore
changed the principal model to a TextCNN that can be
trained and checked on CPU. This is a deviation from the
specified environment, not a workaround that satisfies it. The
functional and evaluation work is complete, but we make no
claim of having completed the assessed remote Linux GPU
practice.
II. PROBLEM DEFINITION AND OBJECTIVES
Each utterance is treated as a 151-way single-label problem:
one of 150 supported intents, or OOS. The intended users are
operators and engineers working with a constrained assistant.
The system returns a routing decision only; it does not
generate an answer or control equipment. An unsupported or
ambiguous request should be clarified or passed to a person,
not forced into the closest available intent.
We had five objectives: fit a credible baseline without using
validation or test data to learn features; compare M0, M1, and
M2 on the same splits and metrics; measure overall and
in-scope performance together with OOS precision, recall,
F1, and AUROC; test deterministic spelling noise; and
inspect representative errors, including cases where R-Drop
changes the prediction. Macro-F1 is used for validation
selection. The test set is evaluated once, after all six M1/M2
runs and every configuration have been frozen.
III. DATASET DESCRIPTION AND
GOVERNANCE
CLINC150 contains 23,700 English utterances covering 150
supported intents and OOS[1]. We use the UCI data_full
release and keep the original partitions: 15,100 training,
3,100 validation, and 5,500 test examples[2]. Each supported
intent has 100 training examples, with a further 100 OOS
examples. Validation has 100 OOS cases, but the test set has
1,000. OOS estimates are therefore less stable on validation
data and should not be expected to transfer exactly to test.
The two official sources do not report the same license
version. UCI's metadata lists CC BY 4.0, whereas the
LICENSE file inside the downloaded bundle states
Attribution 3.0 Unported. The package retains that file and
records the discrepancy rather than silently selecting one
version. The dataset and paper are cited in all distributed
materials.

All queries are English and single-intent. Crowd workers
paraphrased seed phrases or responded to scenarios[1]. The
benchmark therefore captures useful lexical variation but not
multi-intent commands, dialogue context, accented speech
transcripts, or specialized maintenance vocabulary. OOS is
heterogeneous rather than a coherent semantic class, so a
model may learn the observed unsupported style without
covering every possible unsupported request.
The audit found three exact or normalized texts shared
between train and validation and two shared between train
and test. Four normalized texts have conflicting labels across
splits. For example, “what is on my to do list” is associated
with reminder in validation but todo_list in training. These
examples are retained to preserve the official benchmark and
are reported as leakage and annotation limitations rather than
silently removed.

IV. METHODOLOGY
A. M0: TF-IDF logistic regression
M0 combines word 1–2 gram and character 3–5 gram
TF-IDF features with multinomial logistic regression[3]. Both
vectorizers use sublinear term-frequency scaling, lowercase
text, Unicode accent stripping, minimum document
frequency two, and at most 50,000 features each. Their union
produces 32,755 features. Logistic regression uses C=1.0,
lbfgs, at most 300 iterations, and seed 42. Feature fitting
occurs on training data only.
B. M1: compact TextCNN
M1 applies convolutional filters of widths 3, 4, and 5 to
100-dimensional trainable word embeddings, with 100 filters
per width. ReLU activation and max-over-time pooling
produce a 300-dimensional sentence representation, followed
by dropout 0.5 and a 151-way linear classifier. This design
models local word-order patterns while remaining small
enough for exact CPU reproduction[4]. Utterances are
truncated or padded to 32 tokens, and padding is masked
during pooling. The realized model has 453,251 parameters.
M1 is trained for at most 12 epochs with batch size 64,
AdamW, learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.0001, 5% linear
warmup, and gradient clipping 5.0. Early stopping uses
validation Macro-F1 with patience three. M1 and M2 use the
same vocabulary, initialization policy, data order, optimizer,
scheduler, and evaluation code. Each is run with seeds 13, 42,
and 87. No pretrained model or external API is required.
C. M2: one controlled R-Drop modification
M2 changes only the training objective. Each training
utterance passes through the dropout-enabled network twice.
The objective is the mean cross-entropy over the two
predictions plus a symmetric Kullback–Leibler consistency
term[5]. An initial weight of 4.0 reduced validation Macro-F1
to 0.8304 and OOS F1 to 0.2247 for seed 13; it was therefore
rejected using validation evidence only. Weight 1.0 gave a
small clean-score trade-off but improved 5% and 10%
validation-noise Macro-F1, so it was frozen for all remaining
seeds. No test result informed this choice.
D. Evaluation and reproducibility
Overall accuracy measures the total decision rate, while
Macro-F1 treats each label equally. Separate in-scope metrics
prevent OOS behaviour from being hidden. OOS precision,
recall, F1, and AUROC are reported together because
ranking quality and default decision quality differ. Three
paired seeds provide mean and standard deviation, not a
claim of statistical significance. Every run stores
configuration snapshots, data hashes, epoch history,
environment records, best checkpoints, and predictions. Final
test commands require explicit confirmation and all six
frozen checkpoints.

B. Validation evidence and test discipline
Mean validation Macro-F1 was 0.8723 ± 0.0046 for M1 and
0.8713 ± 0.0030 for M2. R-Drop was slightly lower on
average, with improvements in some seeds and declines in
others. We therefore report the positive final-test differences
as observations, not as an outcome guaranteed by validation.
No R-Drop weight or model choice was changed after seeing
the 5,500-row test results.
C. Spelling-noise robustness
We generated the noise by deleting letters, swapping adjacent
letters, and substituting letters with fixed per-row seeds.
Every model receives exactly the same corrupted utterance.
We evaluate 5% and 10% noise on the frozen test set without
further training or threshold adjustment. This is a test of
surface corruption only; it says little about paraphrases,
domain shift, or deliberately adversarial input.

D. Systematic error analysis
Most large confusion groups in the predeclared seed-42
analysis are OOS utterances accepted as supported intents.
The most frequent are OOS→recipe (33 cases),
OOS→definition (18), OOS→smart_home (17), and
OOS→weather (17). Unsupported requests can therefore
share strong lexical cues with valid functions. Errors within
the supported classes look different:
calendar→calendar_update, for example, hinges on whether
the user wants information or a change.
The paired examples help explain R-Drop's mixed effect. For
“what does post rush hour traffic generally look like heading
into the lincoln tunnel in manhattan from new jersey”, M1
predicts OOS with probability 0.717, but M2 correctly
predicts traffic with probability 0.939. For “this song needs to
be added to my country music playlist”, M1 correctly
predicts update_playlist, whereas M2 predicts play_music
with probability 0.778. R-Drop fixes some errors and
introduces others, sometimes with high confidence. A
confidence score by itself is not a safety guarantee.
Some disagreements also come from the data. “what is on my
to do list” has different labels in different splits, so it should
not be counted as an ordinary modelling mistake without
comment. We include row-level predictions, shared-error
tables, and lists of errors corrected or introduced by M2 so
that readers can inspect these cases directly.
VI. DISCUSSION AND RESPONSIBLE
APPLICATION
There is no single best model in these results. M0 is
inexpensive, interpretable, better on supported intents, and
less sensitive to character noise. TextCNN catches more
unsupported requests. R-Drop gives small, consistent gains in
clean and noisy Macro-F1, at some cost to mean OOS
performance. M0 is the sensible choice when ordinary
command accuracy matters most. M1 or M2 may be
preferable when rejecting unsupported input is more
important, provided the lower in-scope score and remaining
OOS errors are acceptable.
These results do not justify deployment in a factory.
CLINC150 contains general assistant queries, not
maintenance procedures, equipment alarms, safety interlocks,
or engineering abbreviations. A real deployment would need
representative approved data, another bias and leakage audit,
and tests of multilingual input and speech-recognition errors.
It would also need visible scope limits, calibrated rejection,
and a safe human fallback for high-impact actions.
We retain the dataset paper and both license statements in the
package. The repository contains no credentials, private
absolute paths, restricted data, or large generated
checkpoints. Examples should be screened before they
appear in screenshots or videos. Future security tests should
include malformed and deliberately misleading requests, not
just random spelling noise. No external API or closed hosted
model is used.
Good results do not remove the compliance issue. The course
requires principal deep-learning experiments on an approved
remote Linux GPU server, whereas every M1/M2 run used

VII. CONCLUSIONS
M0 is the strongest ordinary classifier, with test Macro-F1
0.8508 and in-scope Macro-F1 0.9148. M1 and M2 offer
better unsupported-request detection, raising OOS F1 from
0.4176 to 0.5862 and 0.5746. R-Drop gives small,
seed-consistent gains in clean and noisy Macro-F1, but
slightly lowers mean OOS performance and does not close
the robustness gap to M0. The practical lesson is to select
models against the operating objective rather than a single
score. All results are reproducible on CPU; the approved
Linux GPU practice requirement remains unmet.
